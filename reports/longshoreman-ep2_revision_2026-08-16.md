PASS: pov / voice   PAGES: Ep2

Third close on Joey; a colder, more formal voice than Vinny's — and it holds. No head-hop into
Vinny. Where it slips is *distance*, not person:

1. **L33 — "The reveal giving its intended dramatic effect."**
   → **Rule: the narrator doesn't grade the scene.** In close third the narration is Joey's
   perception; this line is the author stepping in front of him to tell the reader "that worked."
   The very next line — "Joey!" — IS the effect. → *Why it costs:* the reader feels managed
   (room E2-7c). Cut is the fix.
2. **The tell-after-show habit is a POV habit** (E2-7 a/b/d/e): "He was waking up." / "He was
   aghast." / "Vinny was startled." / "The words cut Vinny deep."
   → **Rule: R.U.E. — Resist the Urge to Explain (Browne & King, ch. 1).** When the previous
   sentence showed it (fluttering eyes, the stammer, the exclamation, the sobbing), the
   sentence that names it is a second, weaker copy. → *Why it costs:* every one tells the reader
   you don't trust the image you just wrote — and Joey's voice, which is contemptuous and
   economical, would never explain. **Five in 830 words. This is THE habit to break in this
   voice.** Test: read the sentence before it. If it already did the job, delete.
3. **Filter words (checker: 13).** The ones that matter: L21 "Joey watched his doofus brother,
   Vinny, open the container" (→ *His doofus brother opened the container*); L31 "'Tone!' He heard
   his brother." (→ the shout alone); L37 "Joey could feel the shadows taking root… He could feel
   his weakness" (feel ×2 — and "his" is ambiguous, room E2-4); L43 "He watched Vinny wriggle";
   L51 "He looked around, turning his head violently."
   → same rule as Ep1: the filter narrates the perceiving; cut it and the reader perceives.
4. **Whose darkness?** L37 "The shadows slithered in his darkness" — Joey's or Vinny's? Both
   readings survive. In close third, "his" defaults to the POV character (Joey), which is
   probably not what you mean. → **Rule: an ambiguous pronoun in a two-man scene resolves to the
   POV character; if that's wrong, use the name.**

PASS: line / grammar   PAGES: Ep2

**Repeat offender #1 — the vocative comma (again — 6 times).**
- L75 "Unfortunately, buddy you've gotten…" → "buddy, you've" · L77 "What do you know Joey?" →
  "know, Joey?" · L83 "Look man just fucking relax, man." → "Look, man, just fucking relax, man."
  · L91 "Vinny you can't use magic." → "Vinny, you can't" · L91 "Because, Vin." (correct) ·
  L103 "Ok, Vinny." (correct).
- → **Rule: a name or term of address is set off by commas.** Same note as Ep1; same one-
  keystroke habit. You get it right about half the time — make it all the time.

**Repeat offender #2 — dialogue tags vs. action beats (2 times, and it's a rule you'll use
forever).**
- L93 `“I’m still your big brother.” He sobbed.` · L87 `“Why, me?” He bumbled out between sobs.`
- → **Rule: if the verb describes HOW the words were spoken (said, sobbed, muttered, bumbled),
  it's a TAG — comma inside the quotes, lowercase pronoun: "I'm still your big brother," he
  sobbed. If it's a separate ACTION (He wiped his nose.), it's a BEAT — period, capital.**
- → *Why it costs:* the period + capital tells the reader the sentence ended and a new action
  started; then "He sobbed" turns out to be the speaking. Micro-stumble, twice a page adds up.
  (You do it right at L15: "muttered to himself.")

**Repeat offender #3 — commas between subject and verb (2 times).**
- L11 "Time to make Dad, pay attention." · L37 "Long, withering tendrils, clenched onto his throat."
- → **Rule: never put a single comma between a subject and its verb.** "Time to make Dad pay
  attention." / "Long, withering tendrils clenched onto his throat."
- → *Why it costs:* the reader's ear waits for a second comma that never comes.

**Repeat offender #4 — Dad/dad, Mom/mom (5 times, inconsistent).**
- L11 "Dad" (capital) · L53 "dad's fat face" · L53 "mom's smallness" · L85 "bait for dad" · L89
  "dad only keeps you" · L91 "Dad cares for you" (capital).
- → **Rule: capitalize a family word when it stands in for a name (no "my/his/the" in front):
  "make Dad pay," "bait for Dad," "Dad only keeps you." Lowercase when it has a possessive or
  article: "his dad's face," "Vinny's dad."** Note "dad's fat face" here is a possessive USE
  of the name → "Dad's fat face."
- → *Why it costs:* the same word capitalized two ways in one scene reads as carelessness — and
  Dad is the series' central figure. Decide once.
